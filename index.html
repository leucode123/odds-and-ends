Incidence or prevalence?

Prevalence is a snapshot: everyone who has the disease right now. Incidence is a movie: who becomes a new case over a period. Read each line aloud, let the room shout I or P, then click.

Prevalence
existing cases at a timetotal population at that time

Point or period prevalence. A proportion, 0–1. Measures disease burden → planning beds, chairs, drug supply.

Incidence proportion (risk, cumulative incidence)

A probability, 0–1. Used to study aetiology and risk factors.

Incidence rate (incidence density, hazard)

0 to ∞, units of 1/time. Instantaneous, like speed. In a steady state, 1/rate = average waiting time.

Warm-up · what are odds?

Meet Lucky: water-pistol roulette

Lucky faces a water pistol with 6 chambers. Only 1 is filled with water, at a random spot, and nobody knows which. Every pull turns to the next chamber (no re-spin). Before each pull, ask the room: what are Lucky's odds of getting soaked?

Probabilitywater ÷ all chambers left = favorable ÷ (favorable + unfavorable)
Oddswater ÷ empty chambers left = favorable ÷ unfavorable
Odds this pull (if Lucky is still dry)
Probability this pull (if Lucky is still dry)
conditional probability
Chance, from the start, that it's this pull
unconditional: always 1 in 6
Show the math

Conditional vs unconditional. The odds and probability columns assume Lucky stayed dry on every earlier pull: P(water on pull 3 | dry on pulls 1–2) = 1/4. From the start, before any pull, each pull is equally likely to be the wet one: P(water on pull 3) = 5/6 × 4/5 × 1/4 = 1/6.

Converting. p = odds ÷ (1 + odds), and odds = p ÷ (1 − p).

Why “∞”? 1 ÷ 0 is undefined in ordinary arithmetic. As p gets closer to 1, odds = p ÷ (1 − p) grows without limit, so at p = 1 we say the odds are infinite. Odds have no ceiling; probability stops at 1.

Why it happensOdds = p ÷ (1 − p). When p is small, (1 − p) ≈ 1, so odds ≈ risk and OR ≈ RR (the rare-disease assumption). As p grows, the denominator shrinks and the odds balloon.
OR = RR × (1 − p₀) ÷ (1 − p₁)
Why only odds?

In a case–control study, you choose how many controls exist

Lorenzo-González et al.: 489 lung-cancer cases and 751 controls, non-smokers in NW Spain; exposure = high residential radon (≥200 Bq/m³). Now play investigator: recruit more or fewer controls. Exposure among controls stays at 26%, as it would in the source population.

Watch the control columnWhen you recruit more controls, both control cells grow by the same multiplier. The multiplier cancels in the OR but not in the “risk”:
“Risk” of cancer if exposed
a ÷ (a + b) — meaningless here
“Risk ratio”
moves with your recruiting
Odds ratio
(a/b) ÷ (c/d), unaffected by the case:control ratio
The punchlineThe proportion of cases in the sample is set by the investigator, not by nature, so “risk” of disease cannot be calculated. The OR survives because it can be read in either direction:
(192/297) ÷ (195/556) = (192/195) ÷ (297/556) = 1.84
odds of exposure in cases vs controls = odds of disease in exposed vs unexposed.

Fine print: the OR stays put because we assume the controls are a fair sample of non-cases (26% exposed, whatever their number). In a real study each new control sample gives a slightly different OR (random error; more controls → narrower CI), and unrepresentative controls bias it. What never changes the OR is the case:control ratio itself.

Interpretation check

Controversy & debate · J Clin Epidemiol 2022

Should the relative risk retire?

Doi et al. argued the RR has “questionable utility” and the OR should be the default. Vote before you read the arguments, then vote again.

Votes before arguments: OR 0 · RR 0

Team OR (Doi et al.)

  • RR has a ceiling. It can never exceed 1 ÷ baseline risk. With a 50% baseline, the biggest possible RR is 2, however strong the effect.
  • RR drifts toward 1 as baseline prevalence rises, even when the underlying strength of association is identical (try the slider below).
  • OR is symmetric. OR for the event = 1 ÷ OR for the non-event. RR for death ≠ 1 ÷ RR for survival.
  • OR is a likelihood ratio: the fold increase in odds from baseline to intervention, independent of prevalence.
  • Works in every design (cohort, RCT, case–control) and falls straight out of logistic regression.

Team RR (the usual view)

  • Intuitive. “1.5 times as likely” is what clinicians and patients understand. Odds are a gambler's unit.
  • OR overstates the effect when the outcome is common, and readers routinely misread an OR as an RR.
  • Collapsibility. Adjusting for a prognostic factor can change the OR even with no confounding; the RR does not have this problem.
  • For trials and cohorts the RR, together with the absolute risk difference (CONSORT), directly describes what happens to patients.
Votes after arguments: OR 0 · RR 0

Same strength of association, different baseline risk

Hold the odds ratio fixed and slide the baseline risk. The RR shrinks toward 1 and bumps its head on a ceiling.

What is p₀?The risk of the outcome in the comparison group (control / unexposed / placebo), before any effect. Example: in the dutasteride trial 25% of placebo men got prostate cancer, so p₀ = 0.25.
What is the ceiling?A risk can never go above 100%. So the most the exposed group can reach is p₁ = 1, which caps the RR at 1 ÷ p₀. If 50% of controls already get the outcome, the RR can be at most 2, however harmful the exposure. The OR has no such cap.

Where the class should landBoth sides agree on one thing: never report a relative measure alone. Report the absolute difference (and NNT/NNH) too, and say clearly whether a number is an OR or an RR.

What is a model? A simple recipe for a messy world

Real patients are messy: no two 60-year-olds have the same blood pressure. A model squeezes that mess into a short rule we can read, use and test. It leaves details out on purpose.

Think of a mapA city map leaves out every tree and car, and that is why it's useful: you can find your way. A model is a map of the data: it keeps the main road (the trend) and drops the small details (the noise).
Say it“All models are wrong, but some are useful.” (George Box). We don't ask whether a model is perfect, only whether it is useful for our question.
What is a statistical model?

Data = signal + noise

Everything before this slide was a crude estimate from a table. A model is a small mathematical recipe for how the world produces the data: a rule (the signal) plus scatter we can't explain (the noise). New patients give new dots; the rule stays the same.

Read the recipe
Y = β₀ + β₁X + e
The rule is what we want to learn. The e is everything else about each patient. Set noise to 0 and the dots sit on the line; turn it up and the rule hides in the cloud.

How do we find the best line?

We use the 4-point example from the slides. Pick a method with the switch below, move the sliders, and watch the scores.

Method 1 · Ordinary least squares (OLS)For each point, measure the miss (y − ŷ), square it and add them all up (SSR). Pick the line with the smallest total. On the plot: the smallest total area of squares.
Method 2 · Maximum likelihood (ML)Put a bell curve around the line at each x and ask: how likely are the points we actually saw? Pick the line that makes the data most likely. On the plot: the tallest bell heights at the dots.
SSR = Σ(y − ŷ)²
OLS: make this small
Log-likelihood
ML: make this big (σ = 1)
Why they agreeWith normally distributed errors, log-likelihood = constant − SSR ÷ (2σ²). The line with the smallest SSR is the line with the largest likelihood. ML is the more general referee: logistic regression has no squares to minimise, so it uses ML.
Other ways people find a line

Eyeballing: what you did above. Not reproducible.

Least absolute deviations (median regression): minimise Σ|e| instead of Σe². Less pulled by outliers.

Gradient descent: start somewhere, take small steps downhill on the SSR bowl. How software fits big models.

Bayesian: combine the likelihood with prior knowledge to get a distribution for β.

The ladder

Probability → odds → log-odds

Each rung removes a wall. Probability lives between 0 and 1. Odds remove the ceiling (0 to ∞). The log removes the floor (−∞ to +∞), which finally matches what a linear equation can produce.

Noticep = 0.5 ↔ odds = 1 ↔ log-odds = 0. And p and 1 − p give log-odds of equal size and opposite sign: the scale is symmetric.
The key idea · the sigmoid (logistic) function

Why model log-odds instead of probability?

We want to use a straight line (β₀ + β₁X) because it is simple. The question is what the straight line should predict.

✗ Straight line for probabilityp = β₀ + β₁X
A line never stops: far enough left it goes below 0, far enough right above 1. Impossible probabilities (the first chart on this tab).
✓ Straight line for log-oddslog-odds = β₀ + β₁X
Log-odds run from −∞ to +∞, so the line can go anywhere and is never wrong. Convert back with the sigmoid and the probability always lands between 0 and 1. Bonus: eβ₁ is the odds ratio.

See the same patients on all three scales: probability is an S stuck between 0 and 1, odds is a J-curve from 0 to ∞, and only log-odds is a straight line. Move β₀, β₁ and X and watch all three move together.

Dots = patients with the same glucose (bigger dot = more patients), shown on each scale. ▲ / ▼ = a group off the chart: when everyone (or no one) in a group has diabetes, its odds is ∞ (or 0) and its log-odds is +∞ (or −∞). That is why we can't just draw a line through these dots, and why the model is fitted by maximum likelihood on the 0/1 data. Press Fit by maximum likelihood and the curves pass through the dots.

Why model the log-odds?

One number, the same everywhere: eβ is the odds ratio

Add 1 mmol/L of glucose anywhere on the curve. The change in probability depends on where you start; the multiplication of the odds never does.

  • No impossible predictions. Log-odds run from −∞ to +∞, like the linear predictor, so back-transformed probabilities always stay in (0, 1).
  • A single effect size. Additive on the log-odds scale means multiplicative on the odds scale: eβ₁ = OR per unit of X, at every X.
  • Symmetric. Model “survived” instead of “died” and the coefficients just flip sign (OR → 1/OR).
  • Valid in case–control samples. Over-sampling cases only shifts β₀; the slopes (ORs) are unchanged. That's the case–control station again.
  • Stable fitting. Log-probability (log-binomial) models give RRs directly but need constraints to keep p ≤ 1 and often fail to converge.
